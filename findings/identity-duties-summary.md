# When does Utah law require identifying a person?

Most people carry an intuition that the state knows who they are — that interacting with government
means being identified, and that the identification gets checked. Utah's law does not work that way,
and the shape of the exceptions is more interesting than the rule.

Three assumptions are worth dislodging before reading the table below.

**"Identify" is four different duties, and Utah mostly imposes the weakest.** There is a difference
between *asserting* an identity (writing a name on a form), *proving* it (validating the claim against
authoritative evidence), *verifying a status* (confirming age or lawful presence, which says nothing
about who you are), and *attesting* to it (swearing, and being punished if you lied). Utah's dominant
design is the last. Its most general benefits provision, §63G-12-402, verifies lawful presence rather
than identity, applies only to applicants 18 or older, exempts emergency care and immunisations and
shelter outright, and for a citizen is satisfied entirely by a signature: the applicant must
"*certify under penalty of perjury*," with the federal SAVE check reaching only the non-citizen
branch. Signing the form *is* the procedure.

**Collection is not correlation — but collection is often mandatory.** A fishing licence form must,
by rule, "*include the licensee's customer identification number, name, date of birth, address,
height, weight, eye color, hair color, gender*" (R657-45-2(2)). An agency that failed to gather that
would be out of compliance. Nothing requires anyone to check a word of it. Writing down identifying
facts lays a foundation for correlation without amounting to it, unless someone must submit or
compare what they gathered.

**Where strong identification does exist, the verifier is usually not the state.** Selling a house
requires photo ID — presented to a *notary*. Buying alcohol requires proof of age — checked by a
*retailer*. Enrolling a child requires a birth certificate — produced by a *parent*. "Utah law
requires strong identification" and "a state agency must strongly identify you" are different
propositions, and the second is markedly rarer. Where the state itself proofs, it is overwhelmingly
under **federal conditions on federally funded means-tested benefits**, and even then by electronic
match first, with documents only on mismatch.

## The landscape

Postures: **None** · **Collection** (attributes gathered, unchecked) · **Attestation** (sworn,
perjury-backed) · **Attribute** (age or status proved, not identity) · **Document** (a credential
presented) · **Proofing** (identity validated against authoritative evidence).

|---|---|---|

## What the pattern says

Verification appears where the state fears **systemic** gaming — ballot access, title to land,
federally funded benefits, firearms, and the driver-licence credential that so much else leans on. It
is absent from the ordinary transactional interactions that make up most of civic life: buying a
licence, paying a fine, filing a petition, requesting a record, forming a company. In those, Utah
deters the lie afterward rather than preventing it at the counter — a complete and coherent legal
design, not a weak approximation of proofing, and not non-compliance.

Two structural features follow. The **verifier is frequently a private party the state has deputised**
— notary, retailer, licensee — so the duty exists without the state ever performing it. And a
substantial body of law runs the **opposite** way, protecting anonymity affirmatively: donors,
whistleblowers, crisis-line callers, safe-haven parents, voters. A state identity system has to
accommodate interactions the state is forbidden to trace.

Utah therefore has **no general identity-assurance baseline**. Assurance is set interaction by
interaction and ranges from nothing, through collection and attestation, to biometric proofing. The
claim that the state must strongly identify people generally is not supported; the claim that it
never does is equally wrong. The dividing line is not compliance — it is what kind of risk the
legislature was addressing.
